0. Methodology

Status: proposal (for discussion)

D2 is a living document. It does not try to survey everything at once. It covers a small number of key subjects, each answered in the same way, and grows as members contribute more subjects.

Subjects

A subject is a question an enterprise practitioner has to answer to run agentic systems reliably, for example "How do I keep a long-running agent task alive across pod loss and rolling updates?" Each subject is written up as one question card.

A subject belongs in D2 when it:

  • threatens the outcome of an enterprise use-case pattern (§4) or a requirement (§5);
  • is about evaluating, detecting, verifying or recovering from failures of accuracy or reliability;
  • stays within the charter scope: not model training, not vendor selection, not building a platform.

The first iteration starts with durable execution (Q-PRD-01) and side-effect safety (Q-PRD-02). Further subjects are contributed by WG members (see Contributing in the README).

Lifecycle

Every subject is placed on the lifecycle of an agentic system, so that evidence can be followed from one stage to the next:

Stage Question it answers
Research and selection Is this model, framework or approach fit for the task?
Development and integration Is it built and wired so that it can be reliable?
Pre-production validation Does it hold up under test, perturbation and failure?
Deployment and operations Can we see and verify that it works in production?
Recovery and improvement Can it recover, and do incidents become tests?

§6 gives the detail for each stage. A recurring gap is that evidence does not carry from one stage to the next.

Method for each subject

  1. Ask. State the question, the use-case pattern and the requirement behind it, and the lifecycle stage (above).
  2. Map. Assemble what exists today, open source first: frameworks and harnesses, protocols, runtimes and orchestrators, evaluation and observability tooling, standards, practices, and benchmarks and leaderboards where they exist. Benchmarks are one input among these.
  3. Name the gaps. Compare the landscape against the requirement and record each gap using the categories in §8, with the layer it sits in.
  4. Hands on. Where feasible, check a key claim with a small experiment or test rather than documentation alone, and record the result, or why it was not possible.

Evidence rules

  • Every statement about what exists links to a source (documentation, specification, repository, paper) and notes the version or date consulted.
  • A statement that nothing exists says how and when it was searched, and is worded as "none found", not "none".
  • Experiments state the setup, the version tested and the number of runs; small runs are labelled illustrative.
  • Terms come from D1; unclear terms are fed back to D1.

Keeping it alive

  • One subject or one card per pull request, with a named owner.
  • Cards carry a status (draft, reviewed) and the date of their last landscape check.
  • §7 and §8 are syntheses across cards. They are updated as cards are added or refreshed, not written separately.
  • The WG reviews the card list periodically to add subjects, retire stale ones and refresh landscape entries.

2. Purpose and Audience

Status: outline (carried over from the running notes)

Primary audience: enterprise practitioners responsible for evaluating, deploying, operating, governing, or adopting agentic systems, who need to translate current benchmark research into practical reliability and accuracy decisions inside their organizations.

One style could be reader personas (builders, evaluators/data scientists, product owners, operators/SREs, governance/risk teams, buyers/adopters) and the questions they need answered.

Charter objectives

The charter asks D2 to survey the state of the art, identify which accuracy and reliability dimensions are underserved, and directly inform D4. This breaks down into four objectives:

  1. Assemble the landscape. Map what enterprises can build on today, with emphasis on open source: agent frameworks and harnesses, protocols, runtimes and orchestrators, evaluation and observability tooling, standards, and benchmarks and leaderboards. Benchmarks are one lens on this landscape, not the whole survey.
  2. Identify underserved dimensions. For each accuracy and reliability dimension (terms from D1), show what the landscape covers and where it does not, including gaps in how the pieces fit together (see §8 for gap categories).
  3. Prioritize for enterprise deployment. Rank gaps by their impact on the enterprise use-case patterns (§4) and requirements (§5), and on whether open-source options can close them.
  4. Inform D4 and hand off the rest. Give D4 the evidence it needs on evaluation dimensions and measurement, and route remaining gaps to D1, D3, D5, D6 and inter-WG liaison, or mark them out of scope (§9).

3. Analysis Scope and Evaluation Subjects

Status: outline (carried over from the running notes)

  • Covers a small set of key subjects (starting with durable execution and side-effect safety) contributed by WG members, selected and analysed as described in §0 Methodology. New subjects are added over time.
  • Analyzes benchmark and evaluation method coverage for enterprise agentic systems.
  • Adopts D1 terminology for evaluation subjects: model/component, agent, multi-agent system, and workflow for a given evaluation context/envelope. Does not redefine D1 taxonomy, evaluate model internals, or prescribe vendor-specific tooling.

Focus

The landscape of agent frameworks, harnesses, protocols, runtimes and tooling is too broad to survey end to end. D2 addresses a few elements of it, chosen because they decide whether an agentic system keeps working under real conditions, and because the charter's later deliverables (D3 design guidance, D4 quality standard, D5 corrective actions) need evidence on them:

  • Resilience by design. Properties built in before failure happens: durable execution, idempotent side effects, state recovery, retries and timeouts. Starting subjects: durable execution (Q-PRD-01) and side-effect safety (Q-PRD-02).
  • Resilience after the fact. Detecting and recovering from failures during operations, using telemetry: failure detection, escalation, rollback or compensation, and turning incidents into tests.
  • Monitoring. What must be observed to support both of the above. Consistent with the charter, D2 looks at which signals and outcomes need to be observable, not how traces and logs are emitted (Observability WG).

Monitoring differs by aspect, so each subject states what is monitored and how:

Aspect Example signals Typical source
Task outcome Result correct against a trust anchor, task completed Online evaluation, sampled checks
Agent behaviour Tool calls, retries, loops, trajectory deviations Traces
State and side effects Duplicate or partial effects, unrecovered state Application and system-of-record checks
System health Latency, errors, cost, resource pressure Metrics
Drift and degradation Quality change across versions or over time Repeated evaluation, trend analysis
User experience Re-prompts, overrides, abandoned tasks Product telemetry, feedback

Other elements are in scope only when a member contributes a subject that meets the criteria in §0.

Template

  • Use-case pattern and task
  • Evaluation subject
  • Agent/model/configuration version
  • Tools and evidence sources
  • Runtime and policy conditions
  • Human involvement
  • Lifecycle stage
  • Cost and latency constraints, where relevant

4. Enterprise Use Cases → Patterns

Status: outline (carried over from the running notes)

Group use cases by enterprise business task, not industry. Candidate families: knowledge retrieval and synthesis, document processing and review, customer or employee support, software engineering and CI/CD, business operations workflows, risk/compliance/audit, incident response and remediation, and procurement/vendor/entity review.

Patterns

  • Knowledge and Evidence: retrieving, synthesizing, and citing information for business tasks like research, support, and procurement review.
  • Artifact Generation and Review: creating or assessing documents, code changes, or structured outputs for tasks such as document processing, compliance review, and CI/CD.
  • Decision Support: analyzing evidence to recommend actions to humans during risk assessments, audits, and entity reviews.
  • Tool-Mediated Action: executing actions that alter external states for business operations, remediation, and software operations.
  • Delegated Multi-Agent Workflow: transferring work, context, and responsibility between agents for complex support, investigations, and multi-step automation.
  • Long-Running Recoverable Workflow: maintaining state, verifying outcomes, and recovering from interruptions during incident responses, approval workflows, and remediation.

Pattern template

Intended outcome · Evaluation subject(s) · Tools, data, and actors · Acceptance criteria or trust anchor · Main failure conditions · User-visible consequences

Examples so far: lost context, incorrect delegation, duplicate actions, concurrent writes, partial effects, and unverified outcomes.

Gaps impacting enterprise use cases

One of the gaps companies must tackle to properly test the accuracy and reliability of their agentic solutions is reconciling the consumer and producer points of view on those concepts. With today's testing environments, there is no way to unify the viewpoints of consumers (typically users prompting agents, or agents interacting with other agents) and producers (the AI system) on accuracy and reliability of generated content. Developers of agentic solutions have all faced agents confidently claiming outcomes that they could easily show to be inaccurate.

Since the business objective of agentic providers is to improve trust between consumers and producers, companies must develop frameworks to reduce this gap. Based on experience with conversational agents for professional software users in a B2B setting, user trust in AI is task/topic specific and built or lost over time. While it depends on the accuracy and reliability of the underlying solution, it also heavily depends on:

  1. Use-case type: assessment depends on how the produced content will be used: explain this to me, show this to me, or do this for me.
  2. Task risk profile: what is the business implication of poor advice if the user acts on it?
  3. User profile: is the user a novice or an expert on the topic?
  4. History of consumer-producer interactions on this topic and use-case type: is there evidence the user trusted the AI on it?
  5. The ability to reconcile the different data and tool contexts with which consumer and producer measure the validity of accuracy and reliability claims.

Companies therefore need analytics frameworks that quantify gaps between consumer and producer quality measures. Users are less likely to engage with AI when the gap between the two perspectives is high, regardless of the producer's scores. Testing therefore needs both benchmarks against set quality metrics and measurement of content delivery in the context of the user's environment.

7. Benchmark Coverage

Status: outline (carried over from the running notes)

Benchmarks are one lens on the landscape, not the whole of it (see §0). This section looks at what benchmarks and leaderboards measure and why their results are hard to use for enterprise decisions. It condenses the benchmark material from the running notes.

Evaluation dimensions

  • Map terms to D1: accuracy, reliability, robustness, recoverability, calibration, etc.
  • Any unclear or contested terminology should be fed back into D1.
Dimension Enterprise meaning
Task accuracy Final state matches the success criteria
Process accuracy Steps and tool order are acceptable
Functional correctness Right tool, valid arguments
Evidence accuracy Claims are faithful to sources and tool output
Contextual correctness Fit for the live user, tenant and policy
Repeated-run success rate Same pass/fail over declared repeats
Trajectory and resource consistency Stable action path, latency, tokens and cost across repeats
Operational fault robustness Behaviour under tool, API and infrastructure faults
Recoverability Retry, fallback and human handoff work without further failure
Calibration Stated confidence matches the empirical error rate
Failure attribution Reasoning vs infrastructure vs tool/world vs user

Benchmarks use overlapping names ("success", "pass", "win rate"). Scores are only comparable once the evaluation envelope is declared: subject, number of repeats, injected faults and agent specification version.

Coverage matrix

  • Summarize current academic and industry benchmark coverage, then use the coverage/gap matrix as the main evidence.
  • For each dimension, compare coverage across model/component, agent, multi-agent system, and workflow. Keep benchmark catalog detail in the appendix.

Why benchmark results do not transfer

Typical public harnesses assume the happy path: an agent (or model) as subject, clean infrastructure, annotated correctness and a single-run success rate.

Gap Enterprise need Benchmark habit
Subject Certify a workflow or multi-agent system Score an agent on isolated tasks
Runs Repeated-run success rate Single-run success as the default
Context User, infrastructure and world evidence User symptoms and faults left out
Governance Declared envelope and failure attribution Leaderboard without a frozen specification

Four lenses explain why academic results are hard to use: requirement coverage (an agent score used to certify a workflow), consumability (no runnable or comparable harness), terminology ("success" without an envelope), and systematic analysis (a single run reported as operational readiness).

Scores should only be compared at the same level of the claim hierarchy: application context, scenario, test, run.

Evidence strength also varies: a declared envelope and identity first, then deterministic oracles (state checks, unit tests), then faults and repeats, then judges and rubrics. Claims at the higher tiers without the first are governance gaps.

Examples (public, not endorsements)

  • tau-bench (arXiv 2406.12045): agent subject, strong repeated-run signal, workflow coverage minimal, envelope often implicit.
  • WebArena (arXiv 2307.13854): agent subject, substantial single-run outcome, weak on reliability and on the user.
  • SWE-bench Pro (arXiv 2509.16941): agent subject, tests functional correctness, no faults and no repeat-run reporting.

Q-PRD-01 · How do I keep a long-running agent run going when the process running it crashes, restarts or is moved?

Stage: Production · Status: partly · Use cases: long-running incident, approval and remediation workflows · Patterns (§4): Long-Running Recoverable Workflow · Requirements (§5): state continuity; concurrency and retry safety; safe retry, resume, rollback or compensation Dimensions (§7): Recoverability, operational fault robustness, repeated-run success rate · Pairs with: Q-PRD-02 (side-effect safety) · Author: Jordan Augé

Challenge

An agent run can last minutes or days. Meanwhile the process running it (the worker) can be interrupted: a crash, a lost machine, a deployment, a scale-down, a timeout. Example: an agent is issuing a refund when its worker dies.

Durable execution records the run's progress as it goes, so a new worker resumes where the old one stopped instead of starting over or repeating work. The software doing this is an engine.

Why this is about accuracy and reliability. A run that restarts from zero may take a different path. A run that repeats a step may charge twice. A run that is silently lost never reports failure. In each case the final answer can look fine while the outcome is wrong, so recovery is a reliability property (can the system get back to a correct state?) and a correctness one (is the outcome still right?).

Agents make this harder than ordinary workflows. A model call is non-deterministic: when an engine replays a step (re-runs it to rebuild where the run was), the answer may change. And a tool call in flight at the crash may or may not have taken effect: the refund may already be done. Its outcome is unknown.

Kubernetes is the main example of a platform that interrupts workers: pod loss, rolling updates, scale-in, eviction. It restarts lost pods but manages pods, not runs: nothing on it saves or restores run state [16][17]. An engine must.

Landscape

Everything below is taken from the cited documentation, read on 2026-11-12. Where we infer something, it says so.

In the AAIF landscape (landscape file of 10 Nov 2026 [24]; the label is the one used there):

  • Google ADK (member): not an engine itself; the ADK integrations page lists a Dapr integration [27] and Temporal documents an ADK integration [26].
  • Diagrid Dapr Runtimes (member): a runtime that includes a workflow engine (durable timers, external events, at-least-once activities) [6].
  • LangGraph (external): checkpointers save graph state so a thread can resume from its last checkpoint [10].
  • MCP (member): the protocol through which agents call tools. It matters here because a tool call is the step whose outcome can be unknown; see Q-PRD-02.
  • OpenTelemetry GenAI SIG (external), Datadog AI Observability (member), Arize AI (external): relevant to monitoring (see below).
  • Microsoft AutoGen (member), CrewAI, LangChain, LlamaIndex (external): agent frameworks and orchestration runtimes. Their behaviour on interruption has not been examined yet.
  • Agent evaluation harnesses (Promptfoo, Braintrust, LangSmith Evaluation): none is known to inject worker interruptions; not checked in detail.

Outside the AAIF landscape:

  • Engines: Temporal, Restate, Dapr Workflow, DBOS Transact and Argo Workflows record progress and resume it, by replaying history or from a journal of completed steps [1][4][6][7][8]. Dapr and Argo are CNCF graduated projects; Temporal, Restate and DBOS are vendor projects [23].
  • Agent frameworks that bring their own persistence or sit on an engine: Dapr Agents (runs model calls as workflow activities [15]), Pydantic AI (supports several engines [14]), kagent [12][13].
  • Platform (where workers run): Kubernetes gives a worker a warning period before it stops and limits how many pods are taken down at once; autoscalers (KEDA) and batch admission (Kueue) add scaling and preemption [16][17][19]. None of them knows about runs.
  • Standards and guidance: the CNCF cloud native agentic standards post and Certified Kubernetes AI Conformance mention agentic workloads; we found no requirement on checkpointing, resume or graceful termination in them [25][28].
  • Evaluation: no public benchmark found for run survival. Fault injection tools can create the failures: Chaos Mesh, Litmus, Toxiproxy [20][21][22].

Today

Teams pick one engine and put each model and tool call in a retryable unit (a step the engine can re-run). Engines are alternatives, and stacking them multiplies retries, so the documented pattern is one framework on one engine [14]. Human approvals and long waits are well covered (external events, signals, timers) [1][6]. Side-effect safety is left to the application, usually through idempotency keys (a unique ID sent with a request, so the receiver can ignore a repeat).

Comparison

Engines differ in what they record and how they resume. Temporal and Dapr replay deterministic code against a recorded history [6]. Restate journals steps [4]. DBOS checkpoints steps in Postgres [7]. Argo re-runs a whole step [8]. Temporal, Dapr and DBOS state that a step may run more than once [1][6][7]: if the worker dies mid-step, the step runs again, so an idempotency key is what prevents a second refund. LangGraph resumes a node (one step of its graph) from its beginning, re-running its model and API calls [10]. kagent restores the last snapshot but does not retry the interrupted turn: that is snapshot-and-revert, not durable execution [12]. Engines and frameworks are alternatives at each layer, not a stack.

How to monitor it

D2 says which signals must be observable; how they are emitted belongs to the Observability WG. For recovery, the questions and the signals that answer them are:

Question Signal to observe Where it can come from
Did the run survive the interruption? Run outcome (completed, failed, stuck) for runs in flight at the time Engine run status; the application
How long did recovery take? Time from interruption to resumed progress (time to safe state) Engine events joined with platform events (pod and node events on Kubernetes [16])
Did it resume in the right place? Resumed step versus last completed step; checkpoint timestamps Engine history or journal
Was the outcome repeated or lost? Count of repeated side effects, compared with the system of record The application's ledger or database, not the agent's own report
Was it one interruption or many? Attempt count and retry source (engine, framework or platform) Engine and framework logs and traces

Sources of signal available today are engine-specific, and which of these each engine and the OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions expose has not been checked. This is one of the gaps below.

Gaps

Gap Type (§8) Layer
No comprehensive, vendor-agnostic overview: guarantees, limits and behaviour on platforms such as Kubernetes are documented per product, and we found no neutral comparison (the landscape above was assembled from each product's own documentation) Coverage Practice
No standard interface: resume, retry and history semantics, and the way a run's state is recorded, differ per engine and framework, so runs and tooling are not portable between them Standardization Interop
Engines and frameworks re-run the step that was in flight at the crash [1][6][7][10], and do not track whether a model or tool call had already taken effect, so external effects are left to idempotency keys set by the application (see Q-PRD-02) Coverage Infrastructure
Overlap with platform and scaling: shutdown timeouts disagree across layers (on Kubernetes: pod, engine, sidecar); eviction, OOM (out-of-memory) kills and preemption bypass graceful termination [18]; autoscalers cannot choose which replica to remove and may ignore in-flight work [19]; engine, framework and platform retries compound [6] Continuity / Depth Infrastructure

Also open, not detailed here: versioning covers code shape but not a changed model, prompt or tool schema under in-flight runs [5].

Next

  • D3: a reference pattern: align the platform's shutdown grace period (on Kubernetes, the pod's) with engine shutdown, and give each run one owner. Longer term, a common vocabulary and interface for durable execution of agents.
  • D4: names and definitions for run survival (share of in-flight runs that complete correctly after an interruption), time to safe state, and checkpoint completeness.
  • D5: recovery rules for interrupted runs.
  • External: bridge to the CNCF AI conformance work, which today says nothing on resume; input to the Observability WG on the signals above.

Requirements and checks

  • REQ-A: an in-flight run completes correctly after a crash, machine loss, deployment, scale-in or timeout (on Kubernetes: pod loss, node drain, scale-in or rolling update).
  • REQ-B: after resume, no completed step is repeated and no side effect is duplicated.
  • TEST-A: kill a worker with no grace period during a step of a 20-step reference run; assert resume at or after the last durable step and zero duplicate effects.
  • TEST-B (Kubernetes case): delete a worker with a grace period and drain its node; assert the signal is handled and the run completes.
  • TEST-C: roll out a new worker version mid-run; assert the run finishes or resumes without non-determinism errors.
  • TEST-D: trigger scale-in and preemption while runs are in flight; assert none is cut off or restarted from the beginning.
  • TEST-E (with Q-PRD-02): partition a worker from the engine, then heal; assert exactly one completion per step.

None of these checks has been run yet.

Q-PRD-02 · How do I make sure an agent's side effects happen exactly once when tools fail or time out?

Stage: Production · Status: partly · Use cases: UC-2 customer refund assistant (also UC-1) · Patterns (§4): Tool-Mediated Action, Long-Running Recoverable Workflow · Requirements (§5): concurrency and retry safety; safe retry/resume/rollback/compensation; outcome verification Pairs with: Q-PRD-01 (durable execution) · Author: Jay Prajapat

Challenge

A refund, cancellation or deployment can succeed while its response is lost (timeout, crash, pod loss). The agent then retries, so the action happens twice, or it reports failure for something that succeeded. The trajectory and the final answer look plausible; only the system of record shows the error.

Illustration (small, n=3 per mode, Google ADK + Gemini, one issue_refund tool): a failure before the refund was handled correctly in 3/3 runs. When the refund succeeded but its response was lost, the reply did not match reality in 3/3 runs and the refund was issued twice in 2/3. This is an illustration, not a benchmark.

Landscape

  • Protocol: MCP ToolAnnotations (idempotentHint, destructiveHint). They are hints only, and the defaults assume a tool is non-idempotent and destructive.
  • Practice: idempotency keys and checking status before retrying, common in payment APIs.
  • Runtime: durable execution engines; retry semantics differ per engine (see Q-PRD-01).
  • Evaluation: fault injection in agent eval frameworks (for example ADK environment simulation, which injects faults before the tool runs); trajectory assertions that can check which tools were called and how often (for example promptfoo trajectory:tool-sequence and trace-span-count, DeepEval); state-based benchmarks (τ²-bench database state checks, AgentDojo).
  • Guidance: OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026 mentions repeated actions only as cascade or cost symptoms.

Today

Teams rely on tool-level idempotency where the backend offers it, and on manual review otherwise. Agent frameworks warn about repeated side effects on resume (for example LangGraph's interrupts docs) but leave handling to developers.

Comparison

Complementary: protocol hints declare intent, durable engines decide when to retry, idempotency keys make retries safe, state-based evaluation verifies the outcome. No single layer covers the problem.

Gaps

Gap Type (§8) Layer
Eval tooling can count tool calls, but none surveyed can inject a failure after the tool commits, or assert on the committed effect (for example "one refund in the ledger") rather than the call count Coverage Evaluation
idempotentHint exists, but client and framework retry behaviour is not specified or testable Standardization / Adoption Protocol
Idempotency conventions for agent tools are ad hoc per backend Standardization Practice
Whether the outcome the agent reports matches the actual outcome is not a standard metric Observability Evaluation

Next

  • D3: a "side-effect-safe tools" pattern (idempotency keys, status check before retry, compensation).
  • D4: outcome-consistency and exactly-once metrics.
  • D5: compensation and escalation when duplicates occur.
  • External: input to the MCP Tool Annotations interest group. Possible link with the Attestation & Evidence group on reported vs actual outcomes.

Requirements and checks

  • REQ-A: at most one side effect per user intent under retries and resumes.
  • REQ-B: the outcome reported to the user matches the system of record.
  • TEST-A: inject a failure after the tool commits; assert side-effect count = 1.
  • TEST-B: compare the agent's final message with the ledger; flag mismatches.
  • TEST-C (with Q-PRD-01): kill the worker mid-tool-call, resume, assert no duplicate.
